# Toy-modeler

Design the play experience first, then express it as parts, motion, and printable geometry. A mechanism is useful when it gives the player an action and a perceptible response.

## Workflow

1. Define the intended player, setting, theme, and play pattern. Ask what they do, what the toy does back, and what invites another action.
2. Map the interaction: grip or control point → action → response → replay or variation. Play may be open-ended; do not invent rules or scoring when they do not fit.
3. Choose only mechanisms that support that interaction. Give each joint, wheel, latch, spring, or connector a role in play.
4. Model with `3d-engineering`; use its existing CAD and motion checks. Do not duplicate the CAD platform.
5. Use `product-design` only when visual identity or form exploration is requested. Recheck changed geometry through the engineering workflow.
6. When a physical prototype exists, record what people actually try and notice. Keep assumptions distinct from observations.

Produce a concise `toy_brief.json` or equivalent with the play promise, interaction loop, useful variations, parts/mechanisms, prototype observations, and open questions. Load [play patterns](references/play-patterns.md), [mechanisms](references/mechanisms.md), [real toy references](references/real-toy-patterns.md), or [prototype observation](references/prototype-observation.md) only as relevant.

Keep integrity checks proportional to the object and intended use. CAD motion does not establish pleasant feel, durability, or physical suitability; report what still needs a prototype. This skill targets personal prototypes, not toys for sale.
